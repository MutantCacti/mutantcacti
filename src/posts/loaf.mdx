title: Loaf

Imagine a god, or a being of total capability. They place a loaf of bread upon a table. They cut the loaf at intervals, so that the loaf becomes a series of slices. Each piece of the bread exists, separate from its neighbours but endowed now with a selfhood that allows it to be measured in relation to the rest. The two ends of the loaf are noted; they have neighbours only on one side. 

Now, say that our god turns their eye from the loaf, and watches instead the world they inhabit; all the slices are still there, in the same order. Imagine that the god refocuses, and decides to count the pieces of the loaf. At a whim, they choose one end and name that piece the first. Its nearest neighbour is the second, then that piece’s nearest neighbour in the same direction is the third. This continues until the final piece is reached, and that piece becomes the final slice.

Imagine a universe. Flames spit from the bellies and skins of stars; plasma churns and burns up the light elements, tears their particles to dust and beats the dust into frenzied dance; light shrieks across empty space. There is the god among the heavens, who walks the long gaps between galaxies and listens close to the music of everything. Under their breath, they mutter endlessly, counting upwards at such speed that the human ear grasps nothing. 

At another time, they began: “one, two, three, ….” Now they are on numbers for which there are no English words; one day they will speak the final one, and it will be inconceivable to all. The god never rests, never flags, never blinks, never makes a mistake. At each number, they see everything as it is, and know it entirely.

Imagine a mortal. The mortal mind, trapped forever in the physical and forced by causality to obey time, sees and hears only a god in the act of counting. At each number—if, by some miracle, they managed to discern each one—they might find themselves in any position, place and act; perhaps, between one number and the ten millionth next number after it, the mortal watches a knife descend into the brown flesh of bread and make a loaf into two slices. 

At each number, the knife was at a different place, and moving at a different speed; the bread was in a different state. The mortal sees this all and thinks: yes, I am moving. Yes, they are moving. Yes, this bread was whole, then it was being cut, then there were two pieces of bread on the table. The mortal speaks its tongue, a tongue which uses words like “was,” and “then,” and “were.” It sees a single universe.

Meanwhile, the god counts. And, on every number, it sees a knife pare down the edge between this universe and the next, a state which, without interval, becomes the next state, the state which every law of nature has discerned must follow immediately after the previous. 

So, the god, muttering under their breath, has seen countless knives cut countless slices from a single loaf; but the loaf is not the universe. No, the universe is one slice of the loaf, the slice upon which our god has laid their attention for this single moment, the one slice identified among its endless neighbours by the number which has just fallen from the god’s lips. 

And the god sees not our universe—they see the whole loaf. For, to them who are not constrained by mortality, the state of the universe at “one,” and the state of the universe at “two,” and “three,” and so on until the uncountably humongous number called “end,” are not divided, lost to the arbitration of what is real and not; they are all present on the table. This god knows time intimately, knows it entirely and sees it for what it is: a way to count bread. 

To the mortal, there is only one slice of bread; the present. Those that have been counted no longer exist; those that have yet to be counted have never existed. This is the false absolution of time, which says that there is a now that is real and an entire loaf of neighbouring ex- and soon-to-be-nows that are not. 

The god knows better; finger over the final slice, it sees the very first it counted lying just a loaf of bread away. All pieces are equally real, all are equally occurring; the illusion of ‘present’ comes only to those whose senses cannot reach beyond the realm of their instantaneous universe, those mortals who could never outgrow the bounds of reality itself. 

The god sees the mortal—they know it is listening to them speak—and they understand it perfectly. It cannot be clearer to the god why the mortal fails to see previous and oncoming pieces as readily as they can: because the god is above and outside the loaf, but the mortal is made of bread.
